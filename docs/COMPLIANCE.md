# Getting legal: federal, Utah, Davis County

Assembled from public guidance and primary sources. **Not legal or tax advice.**
Requirements change and vary by city — confirm anything marked unconfirmed with
the agency itself.

Rates and fees below carry the date they were checked. Sales tax rates in
particular change quarterly.

---

## Do these in this order

### 1. Before you sell anything

**Separate bank account.** Not legally required for a sole proprietor, and the
highest-value thing on this list anyway. Mixed personal and business money is
what turns a routine examination into a painful one, and it is what most often
defeats the argument that a personal collection is separate from inventory.

**EIN.** Free, ten minutes, at irs.gov. You may use your Social Security number
instead, but an EIN keeps your SSN off forms you give to suppliers, consignors
and marketplaces. Most banks want one.

**Decide what is inventory and what is your collection, and write it down.**
See below — this is the decision with the largest dollar consequences.

**Write a de minimis capitalisation policy.** One page, saying you expense items
under $2,500. It has to be in place at the *start* of the tax year, so writing
it in January is worth more than remembering it in April.

### 2. Utah registration

**DBA** — only if you trade under a name that is not your own legal name.
$22 to register, $18 to renew, and it runs **three years**, not one.
Utah Division of Corporations. (Several popular guides quote $27 for renewal;
the Division's own fee schedule says $18.)

**Sales tax licence — form TC-69, free, at tap.utah.gov.**
Storing inventory at home is a physical presence in Utah, so the $100,000
economic nexus threshold does not shelter you — that test is for out-of-state
sellers. You need this as soon as you make a taxable retail sale in Utah that a
marketplace does not handle, which in practice means your first cash sale at a
show. The licence is not transferable.

**Resale exemption certificate — form TC-721.** Give it to suppliers so you are
not paying sales tax on stock you are going to resell. Genuine resale inventory
only: not supplies you use up, and not a card you decide to keep.

### 3. Your city

**A city business licence is probably required, including for a home business.**
Fees and rules differ city by city across Davis County, and this is the
requirement online sellers most often miss because nothing about selling on eBay
feels like it should involve city hall.

**Home occupation rules** typically forbid customer traffic and signage, limit
the share of the home used, and restrict outside employees and deliveries. A
card business in a spare room usually fits comfortably — but the permit is often
still required.

**Check with your own city.** A neighbouring city's rules do not apply to you.

### 4. Ongoing

- Quarterly estimated federal tax (Form 1040-ES)
- Utah sales tax returns on your assigned schedule, including zero returns
- Annual federal return with Schedule C and Schedule SE
- Annual Utah return (TC-40)
- Business personal property tax with Davis County, if you own equipment

## Inventory or collection? The decision that matters most

A card sold **as inventory** is ordinary business income: Schedule C, plus
self-employment tax at 15.3%.

The same card sold **from a personal collection** is a capital gain: Form 8949,
**no self-employment tax**, and if held more than a year the rate is capped at
28%.

On a $2,400 gain the self-employment tax difference alone is roughly $339,
before any difference in rate.

**One person can hold both.** Character is tested asset by asset at the moment
of sale, not once for the whole person. But the burden is on you, there is no
statutory safe harbour for collectibles the way there is for securities dealers,
and it is won or lost on documentation.

**What holds it up, in descending order of weight:**

1. **Separate custody.** Different storage, different insurance, a personal
   account that never touches the business account. Never buy a collection piece
   with the business card; never deposit a collection sale into the business
   account.
2. **A dated written schedule** of the collection, created at acquisition —
   each card, when you got it, what it cost, where from, and that it is held for
   investment. Signed and dated. CardLedger generates this; print it.
3. **Never list it for sale.** No listing, no show table, no stream. Sales
   effort is the factor that most often kills it.
4. **Never run it through inventory.** Its cost is not in purchases, and it is
   not in year-end inventory.
5. **Hold it.** Long holds and few sales look like investment. Buying and
   flipping similar cards does not.

**There is no step-up when a personal card becomes inventory.** Basis carries
over, and the entire gain — including everything that accrued before the
business existed — becomes ordinary income subject to self-employment tax.

### Selling a collection piece to fund the business

A common plan, and the sequencing is worth real money.

Selling it **before the business begins**, in your own name, from your own
account, held more than a year: capital gain on Form 8949, collectibles rate
capped at 28%, no self-employment tax. At a modest income the 28% cap is not
even binding — you pay your ordinary rate.

Selling it **after the business is running**, on the same account, alongside
flips: the argument that it is not stock in trade gets much weaker, and if it
loses, the whole gain becomes ordinary income with self-employment tax on top.

The practical sequence: sell it first, document when you acquired it and what it
cost, report it on Schedule D, then contribute the after-tax cash to the
business as owner's capital. The sequencing costs nothing and can be worth
several hundred dollars.

**This specific situation has not been litigated on these facts.** The closest
authority favours the taxpayer, but it is a facts-and-circumstances question on
which examiners can differ. Paper the position with a CPA *before* you sell, not
after.

## Federal deadlines

| What | Form | When |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated tax, instalment 1 | 1040-ES | 15 April |
| Estimated tax, instalment 2 | 1040-ES | 15 June |
| Estimated tax, instalment 3 | 1040-ES | 15 September |
| Estimated tax, instalment 4 | 1040-ES | 15 January following |
| 1099-K arrives from platforms | 1099-K | Late January |
| Annual return | 1040, Sch C, Sch SE | 15 April following |

Dates shift to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or holiday.

**The fourth instalment can be skipped** if you file the return by 1 February
and pay the balance with it.

**An extension gives more time to file, never more time to pay.**

## Reconciling a 1099-K

Platforms report **gross** payments — including sales tax they collected and
shipping the buyer paid, and **before** their fees and any refunds. That figure
will be higher than what you banked, sometimes by a lot.

The return has to reconcile to it:

- Report the **gross** on Schedule C line 1, including shipping the buyer paid.
- Deduct the platform's fees on line 10.
- Deduct the postage you bought.
- Put refunds on line 2 as returns and allowances.

Do not report the net payout as your income. That is the single most common
mistake, and it makes your return disagree with a form the IRS already has.

Note that **card-reader sales have no 1099-K threshold at all** — payment card
transactions are reported from the first dollar.

## Things that are not deductible

- Federal income tax and self-employment tax (half of SE tax is an adjustment
  elsewhere, not a business expense)
- Entertainment — 100% disallowed, with no 50% fallback
- The cost of inventory you have not sold yet
- Commuting from home to a regular workplace
- A home office space that is not used *exclusively* for the business
- Health insurance on Schedule C line 15 — that line is explicitly "other than
  health", and it goes elsewhere

Donating a card to charity is limited to the **lesser of its value or its
basis** — donating a $2,600 card with $150 of allocated basis gives a $150
deduction, and only if you itemise.

## Sales tax at shows

Utah sources a sale at a temporary event to **the event location**. A Layton
seller at a Sunset show charges Sunset's rate.

Davis County rates, effective 1 July 2026, confirmed unchanged through the end
of the year:

- **7.25%** — Bountiful, Centerville, Clearfield, Clinton, Farmington,
  Kaysville, Layton, North Salt Lake, South Weber, Syracuse, West Bountiful,
  Woods Cross
- **7.15%** — Fruit Heights, Sunset, West Point, unincorporated Davis County

The only difference is a 0.10% municipal cultural and zoo tax that twelve of the
fifteen cities levy.

Rates change quarterly. Re-check the Tax Commission chart before an event.

**Collected sales tax is a trust fund tax.** You hold it for the state, personal
liability attaches, and it should never be spent as working capital.
